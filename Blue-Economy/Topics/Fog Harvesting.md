# Defining and Describing Fog Harvesting

 ![Large mesh fog collectors on a coastal or mountain ridge, with droplets draining into collection gutters](https://media.news.climate.columbia.edu/wp-content/uploads/2011/03/FogQuest9s-600x450.jpg)

- _Fog harvesting turns moving mist into a local water supply with remarkably little energy._
- Fog harvesting is a passive atmospheric-water-collection method in which mesh or other structured surfaces intercept microscopic liquid droplets suspended in fog. [^0mu29b] [^wgkr5g] The droplets coalesce, run downward into gutters, and are conveyed to tanks for filtration, storage, and distribution. [^wgkr5g] [^6tj3sw] It is most applicable in arid or semi-arid coastal and mountainous regions with frequent, wind-driven fog, but its output is limited by local fog frequency, wind conditions, collector design, and water-quality requirements. [^ctz5qb] [^ook80e]

# Uses in Context

- **Drinking-water supply:** The term describes systems that collect fog for household use, generally after UV, sand, cartridge, or comparable treatment. [^wgkr5g] [^6tj3sw]
- **Climate adaptation:** Fog harvesting is invoked as a nature-based response for water-stressed coastal and highland communities. [^ook80e]
- **Low-energy atmospheric-water collection:** Comparative reviews describe it as the lowest-energy atmospheric-water option, while emphasizing its dependence on persistent fog. [^ctz5qb]
- **Community infrastructure:** Projects use “fog farms” or arrays of collectors to provide water where conventional pipelines or groundwater are unreliable. [^3m9dwn] [^sf89sj]
- **Biomimetic engineering:** Researchers use plant-inspired surfaces, hydrogels, and other structured materials to improve droplet capture and drainage. [^71flen]

# History of Use

## Origins

- Traditional practices collected water dripping from vegetation: reports describe “fountain trees” in the Canary Islands and cisterns placed beneath trees in Oman. [^w7gjnt]
- Systematic scientific investigation began in the early twentieth century; researchers in South Africa modified rain gauges between 1901 and 1904 to measure water captured from fog by vegetation. [^w7gjnt] [^sf89sj]
- By 1969, researchers had developed plastic screens designed to “intercept” fog droplets, establishing the basic engineering principle used by modern mesh collectors. [^w7gjnt]
- Practical large-scale projects emerged in coastal Chile and Peru during the 1970s and 1980s, demonstrating that fog could provide a viable water source in arid regions. [^0mu29b]

## Evolution

- **1950s–1969 — From observation to measurement:** Researchers in Antofagasta developed droplet-intercepting nets, while South African work helped formalize methods for measuring fog capture. [^w7gjnt]
- **1987 — Community-scale deployment:** The Chungungo project in northern Chile installed roughly 100 fog collectors for about 300 residents, turning experimental collection into municipal-scale water infrastructure. [^w7gjnt]
- **2010s–present — Integrated social and material design:** Dar Si Hmad’s Morocco project combined large collector arrays with treatment, storage, solar-powered distribution, and community management; newer research adds bioinspired surfaces and membrane or hydrogel materials. [^wgkr5g] [^6tj3sw] [^71flen]

# Case Studies

**Chungungo, Chile.** In 1987, the fishing community of Chungungo in northern Chile installed about 100 fog collectors on the El Tofo ridge. [^w7gjnt] The project produced approximately 33 liters of water per day per resident for a community of roughly 300 people, according to the cited account. [^w7gjnt] Another review describes the broader installation as reaching peak production of about 15,000 liters per day and operating from 1987 to 2001. [^3m9dwn] The case established fog harvesting as more than a laboratory concept: a passive collector network could supplement or replace conventional water delivery in a dry coastal settlement, although long-term viability still depended on maintenance, governance, and reliable fog. [^w7gjnt] [^3m9dwn]

**Aït Baamrane, Morocco.** Beginning in 2010, the women-led organization Dar Si Hmad developed a large fog-harvesting network in southwestern Morocco. [^wgkr5g] [^6tj3sw] Its system stretches mesh nets perpendicular to prevailing winds; captured droplets drain into gutters, move to storage, undergo UV, sand, and cartridge filtration, and are distributed through a solar-powered network. [^wgkr5g] [^6tj3sw] The project is reported as serving five villages and has become a prominent example of combining passive infrastructure with local institutions and community-oriented water distribution. [^wgkr5g] [^6tj3sw] Its significance lies not only in the collectors but in the integrated model: atmospheric capture, treatment, storage, renewable-energy distribution, and community management operate as one water system. [^wgkr5g] [^6tj3sw]

**Emerging material systems.** Recent research extends fog harvesting beyond conventional plastic mesh by testing membrane, hydrogel, and bioinspired surface designs. [^0mu29b] [^71flen] One reported outdoor study achieved approximately 34 liters per square meter per day while maintaining performance over 20 months of testing. [^71flen] These systems show how materials science may improve capture and drainage, but they do not remove the core geographic constraint: fog harvesting remains most useful where fog is frequent and atmospheric conditions provide enough droplets to collect. [^ctz5qb] [^ook80e] [^71flen]
