# Neocloud Operators

The market category of **Neocloud Operators** has emerged over the past three to four years as a distinct stratum within the broader cloud-infrastructure and AI compute landscape, defined by independent GPU-centric clouds that sit between traditional hyperscalers and bare-metal hosting in their economics, architecture, and go-to-market. These operators are responding to an unprecedented surge in demand for accelerated compute, particularly for generative AI and large-scale machine learning, by assembling specialized clusters of cutting-edge GPUs, networking, and cooling, and packaging them into flexible, high-performance services that undercut hyperscaler pricing while targeting both AI-native and increasingly enterprise workloads. [^q5yz1o] [^30zj83] In parallel, they are experimenting with new capital structures, energy strategies, and vertical integration approaches that make AI infrastructure itself the locus of innovation rather than the commodity substrate it has often been treated as in prior cloud eras. [^q5yz1o] [^mgvm0v] [^7jowi2]

At a global level, AI infrastructure spending reached approximately \( \$318 \) billion in 2025, more than doubling from \( \$153 \) billion in 2024, and is projected by IDC to grow at a five‑year compound annual growth rate of around 31 percent to surpass \( \$758 \) billion by 2029, with accelerated servers accounting for over 94 percent of that total. [^wncds7] [^8f9tye] Against this backdrop, neoclouds represent a fast-growing subcategory of AI-focused cloud infrastructure, with McKinsey estimating that more than 100 neoclouds exist worldwide, 10–15 of which operate at meaningful scale in the United States, and with their footprint expanding rapidly across Europe, the Middle East, and Asia. [^q5yz1o] [^30zj83] The profile that follows captures the state of this category through roughly mid‑2026, focusing on the structural forces that have made neocloud operators salient, the current momentum in their economics and financing, and the key players across three tiers—incumbents, challengers, and innovators—who are shaping where this market goes next.

## Snapshot

_“Neocloud Operators” are the GPU‑first cloud providers and vertically integrated AI data‑center platforms that have arisen in response to a global scarcity of high‑end compute, offering bare‑metal GPU clusters, specialized networking, and turnkey AI stacks at prices often dramatically below hyperscalers._ [^q5yz1o] [^30zj83] _More than 100 such neoclouds now exist worldwide, forming a fragmented but rapidly coalescing category that is beginning to attract enterprise customers, long‑term debt financing, and coverage from major analysts as a distinct layer in the AI infrastructure stack._ [^q5yz1o] [^30zj83]

> “More than 100 neoclouds exist in the world today: Between ten and 15 are operating at meaningful scale in the United States, and their footprint is growing across Europe, the Middle East, and Asia.” [^q5yz1o]

In conceptual terms, this category encompasses independent GPU‑as‑a‑service and AI‑cloud platforms such as Crusoe Cloud, Voltage Park, Together AI, Nebius, Lambda, Runpod, and NexGen Cloud’s Hyperstack, along with closely allied data‑center operators and chipmakers whose products and partnerships define the economics and capabilities of these specialized clouds. [^u31kaf] [^m9wwsl] [^mgvm0v] [^7jowi2] [^nson9i] [^3f02sp] [^2a4pi7] [^ynlu1a] [^lc2qme] [^fru0mo] [^m2wq4p] The timeframe captured here is the period from roughly mid‑2022, when some of the earliest neoclouds like Crusoe Cloud began operating, through the wave of large AI‑infrastructure financings and product launches in 2024–2026, including Nebius’s debut debt facility, Lambda’s $1.008 billion term loan, Together AI’s 36,000‑GPU GB200 cluster, and Voltage Park’s AI Factory launch. [^4umvjt] [^mgvm0v] [^7jowi2] [^nson9i] [^ynlu1a] [^lc2qme] It is worth a reference card now because neocloud operators are not just transient “GPU rental shops” filling a supply gap; they are crystallizing into enduring platforms with distinct architectures, financial models, and regulatory postures that will shape how AI workloads are deployed and governed over the next decade. [^q5yz1o] [^mgvm0v] [^30zj83] [^wncds7] [^8f9tye] [^m2wq4p]

## What is this Market Category?

Neocloud Operators are best defined as independent, GPU‑centric cloud providers that supply high‑performance compute for AI training, fine‑tuning, and inference through a combination of bare‑metal clusters, virtualized instances, and increasingly integrated software stacks, without attempting to replicate the full general‑purpose cloud platforms offered by hyperscalers. [^q5yz1o] [^u31kaf] [^m9wwsl] [^7jowi2] [^3f02sp] [^2a4pi7] McKinsey describes neoclouds explicitly as “independent GPU‑as‑a‑service providers” whose offerings range from GPU rental—often termed bare‑metal‑as‑a‑service (BMaaS)—to cloud‑based virtualized or containerized instances, all optimized for accelerated compute workloads. [^q5yz1o] The problems they solve center on access, economics, and specialization: they provide access to scarce, state‑of‑the‑art GPUs such as NVIDIA H100, H200, and Blackwell; they price that access as much as 75–85 percent below hyperscalers’ GPU rental rates; and they configure clusters and networks specifically for large‑scale distributed training, low‑latency inference, and high‑bandwidth data movement rather than for general web or enterprise workloads. [^q5yz1o] [^u31kaf] [^m9wwsl] [^3f02sp] [^30zj83] [^fru0mo] [^m2wq4p]

From a customer perspective, the primary buyers in this category have initially been AI‑native startups, research labs, and high‑performance computing (HPC) teams that require large numbers of tightly networked GPUs, often on short notice and for intensive training runs, but lack the budget, contractual flexibility, or architectural alignment to use hyperscaler GPU instances at scale. [^q5yz1o] [^u31kaf] [^v4e4z9] [^3f02sp] [^30zj83] [^fru0mo] Voltage Park, for example, explicitly markets its AI cloud to “leading AI labs and fast‑scaling startups” as well as enterprises, offering NVIDIA HGX H100 clusters with 3,200 Gbps Quantum‑2 InfiniBand networking, 99.982 percent uptime in Tier 3+ data centers, and pricing starting at $1.99 per hour for on‑demand H100 nodes with no long‑term contracts. [^u31kaf] [^m9wwsl] Runpod positions itself as a GPU‑only infrastructure provider with community and secure cloud offerings, giving customers fine‑grained control over GPU type, price, and billing model while separating storage costs from compute, which is attractive for experimentation and ephemeral workloads. [^fru0mo] Together AI frames its “AI‑native cloud” as the fastest way to run open‑source models on demand, combining self‑serve GPU clusters with model APIs that abstract away infrastructure management for teams focusing on generative media and large‑language‑model applications. [^3f02sp] [^2a4pi7] [^ynlu1a]

The category explicitly excludes general‑purpose hyperscaler clouds such as those offered by large public cloud providers, even though those platforms increasingly offer advanced GPU instances and AI services. [^q5yz1o] [^30zj83] [^8f9tye] Hyperscalers typically operate full‑stack platforms that include compute, storage, databases, analytics, security, and application services, and their GPU offerings are integrated into these broader ecosystems; neocloud operators, by contrast, concentrate on accelerated compute and closely adjacent infrastructure, often relying on hyperscalers or other providers for non‑GPU components. [^q5yz1o] [^30zj83] [^8f9tye] The category also tends to exclude pure chipmakers and hardware vendors such as NVIDIA, AMD, and Dell, except where those firms operate or co‑brand dedicated AI clouds or clusters as part of a neocloud offering, as is sometimes the case with NVIDIA DGX systems deployed by partners like Crusoe and atNorth. [^u31kaf] [^mgvm0v] [^z5xkd5] [^p7agdw] Traditional colocation and data‑center providers are only included insofar as they build and operate AI‑optimized facilities that are deeply entwined with neocloud clusters, as with atNorth’s Iceland ICE02 site that houses Crusoe’s climate‑aligned GPU data center. [^mgvm0v]

There is, however, a fuzzy and disputed boundary around several edges of the category. One dispute concerns whether vertically integrated AI‑infrastructure platforms like Crusoe, which develop their own energy sources, manufacture components in‑house, and operate data centers powered by renewable energy, should be considered neocloud operators or a distinct “AI infrastructure utility” category. [^mgvm0v] [^z5xkd5] [^7jowi2] Another concerns whether specialized European platforms like NexGen Cloud’s Hyperstack, which emphasizes regulatory compliance and green energy for GPU clouds, belong squarely in the neocloud category or in an emerging “sovereign AI cloud” category focused on data residency and local control. [^m2wq4p] Analysts also debate whether certain hyperscaler‑aligned entities, such as Nebius—which has more than $40 billion in contracted revenue from investment‑grade customers including Microsoft and Meta—are best understood as independent neoclouds or as extensions of incumbent cloud ecosystems. [^lc2qme] These boundary disputes underscore that “Neocloud Operators” are not defined solely by technology but also by business model, capital structure, and geopolitical positioning.

## Why Now?

The rise of Neocloud Operators is the product of several converging forces that have made accelerated compute both scarce and central, and that have reshaped willingness among capital providers and customers to contract with non‑hyperscaler clouds. The first and most salient force is the global scarcity of high‑end GPUs and other AI accelerators, particularly in the 2022–2024 period when demand for NVIDIA A100 and H100 chips far outstripped supply. [^q5yz1o] [^30zj83] McKinsey explicitly notes that the emergence of neoclouds “is a direct response to two structural forces: a global scarcity of high‑end compute, and the revenue diversification strategies of the largest advanced-chip producers.” [^q5yz1o] As generative AI workloads ballooned, hyperscalers and major AI labs competed for limited allocations of top‑tier GPUs, leaving both smaller customers and new AI startups unable to secure sufficient capacity through traditional channels. [^q5yz1o] [^30zj83] Into this gap stepped neoclouds, which secured their own GPU inventories—often via close partnerships with NVIDIA and, more recently, AMD—and offered them to third parties via rental and cloud models, effectively arbitraging scarcity while creating new routes to AI acceleration. [^u31kaf] [^mgvm0v] [^p7agdw] [^3f02sp] [^30zj83] [^ynlu1a]

A second enabling force is the rapid and sustained growth of global AI infrastructure spending, which has validated long‑term investments in specialized AI data centers and GPU clusters beyond the hyperscaler context. [^wncds7] [^8f9tye] IDC reports that worldwide spending on AI infrastructure reached $89.9 billion in the fourth quarter of 2025, a 62 percent year‑over‑year increase from Q4 2024, with full‑year 2025 spending totaling $318 billion—more than double the $153 billion recorded in 2024. [^wncds7] In the second quarter of 2025 alone, organizations increased spending on compute and storage hardware for AI deployments by 166 percent year‑over‑year, reaching $82.0 billion, with servers accounting for 98 percent of AI‑centric spending and servers containing embedded accelerators—primarily GPUs—accounting for 91.8 percent of server AI infrastructure spending. [^8f9tye] IDC projects that AI infrastructure spending will reach $487 billion in 2026 and grow at roughly 31 percent CAGR to exceed $758 billion by 2029, with accelerated servers reaching 94.3 percent of total spending and the United States maintaining a leading share. [^wncds7] [^8f9tye] This scale and growth give neocloud operators confidence that demand for accelerated compute is not a temporary bubble but a secular trend, justifying large capital outlays in GPUs, cooling, energy, and software.

A third critical force is a shift in the capital‑formation landscape, in which both credit markets and equity investors have become comfortable underwriting AI infrastructure as a long‑lived asset class tied to contractual cash flows rather than speculative demand. [^mgvm0v] [^nson9i] [^lc2qme] Lambda, for instance, closed a $1.008 billion investment‑grade delayed draw term loan in 2026, marketed to insurance companies and fixed‑income investors, to fund GPU infrastructure for three committed customer deployments with two investment‑grade offtakers across multiple data centers. [^nson9i] The facility, rated A (low) by Morningstar DBRS and Baa1 by Moody’s and priced at a 6.78 percent fixed rate with semi‑annual coupons, is secured by funded GPU servers, related infrastructure, and contracted cash flows, and matures in 2033, underscoring that investors view these clusters as durable assets. [^nson9i] Nebius similarly entered its first senior secured debt facility of approximately $775 million in July 2026, backed by deployed GPU infrastructure and cash flows under an agreement with an investment‑grade customer, with the contract and facility together covering more than 100 percent of the capital expenditure required to deploy the underlying GPUs. [^lc2qme] Crusoe’s expansion of its clean‑energy‑powered data center in Iceland, supported by a $175 million credit facility from Victory Park Capital, likewise signals growing readiness among lenders to finance AI‑optimized data centers that blend energy projects with compute assets. [^mgvm0v] These deals provide templates for asset‑level financing and securitization of GPU clusters, enabling neoclouds to scale capacity quickly while managing equity dilution.

A fourth enabling force lies in technological advances in both hardware and data‑center design that make it feasible to operate extremely dense GPU clusters outside hyperscaler campuses. Crusoe’s data centers, for example, are purpose‑built for intensive AI workloads, with high‑density racks supporting the latest GPUs such as NVIDIA GB200 and AMD MI355x, advanced cooling technologies including direct liquid‑to‑chip, and orchestrated renewable power sources paired with on‑site backup to maintain reliability. [^mgvm0v] [^z5xkd5] [^p7agdw] Voltage Park’s infrastructure similarly combines Dell PowerEdge XE9680 servers packed with NVIDIA HGX H100 GPUs, 1 TB of RAM, and 3,200 Gbps NVIDIA Quantum‑2 InfiniBand networking, housed in Tier 3+ data centers engineered for 99.982 percent uptime. [^u31kaf] Together AI’s clusters are designed “by AI researchers for AI innovators,” incorporating Blackwell Tensor Core GPUs, Grace CPUs, NVLink, InfiniBand, and proprietary kernel optimizations such as FlashAttention to maximize effective bandwidth and training efficiency. [^3f02sp] [^ynlu1a] These hardware and architectural innovations reduce the operational overhead required for non‑hyperscaler entities to stand up world‑class AI clusters, making it possible for neocloud operators to deliver performance comparable to or better than hyperscalers for specific workloads.

A fifth force is the evolution of customer expectations around pricing transparency, security, compliance, and data governance in AI clouds, which has opened space for differentiated offerings. [^m9wwsl] [^v4e4z9] [^30zj83] [^fru0mo] [^m2wq4p] SemiAnalysis notes that most neocloud giants and emerging neoclouds do not charge egress fees for moving data to another GPU cloud, and often bundle CPU, networking, power, local NVMe storage, and job scheduling tools like Slurm into all‑inclusive GPU rental prices, in contrast to hyperscalers’ more complex fee structures. [^30zj83] At the same time, the firm observes a long tail of emerging neoclouds lacking basic SOC 2 or ISO 27001 certifications, which is a barrier to enterprise adoption and forces customers to favor more mature players such as CoreWeave. [^30zj83] Voltage Park explicitly counters these concerns by emphasizing SOC 2 Type II and HIPAA compliance, dedicated virtual private cloud deployments, and more than 200 controls for privacy and security in its enterprise AI Factory offering. [^m9wwsl] [^4umvjt] NexGen Cloud’s Hyperstack positions itself as a European GPU‑accelerated cloud solution that runs on 100 percent renewable energy and mitigates the “regulatory and compliance risks of US‑based alternatives” for European scale‑ups. [^m2wq4p] Crusoe, for its part, foregrounds climate alignment and responsible energy sourcing, arguing that “aligning the future of computing with the future of the climate” is now a competitive differentiator. [^mgvm0v] [^7jowi2] [^p7agdw] Together, these shifts in customer priorities—towards transparent pricing, security, and sustainability—have created niches that neocloud operators can credibly fill.

Finally, there is an important cultural and ecosystem force in the form of open‑source AI and the operator‑thinker community, which has lowered barriers to building on non‑hyperscaler clouds. Together AI explicitly focuses on open‑source models and inference research, offering drop‑in API compatibility and serverless deployments that make it easy for teams to run models without managing infrastructure. [^3f02sp] [^2a4pi7] Crusoe’s origin story emphasizes how the company built a “neocloud” not as a follower but from first principles, driven by an architectural commitment to climate alignment and high‑performance AI, and shared lessons from building containerized data centers in Montana to large‑scale facilities in Iceland. [^7jowi2] SemiAnalysis’s ClusterMAX rating system provides sophisticated methodological tools for evaluating GPU clouds, including neoclouds, across audit, performance, reliability, and fault‑tolerance dimensions, thereby professionalizing the market and enabling informed comparison among providers. [^6gpbyh] [^30zj83] These intellectual and technical frameworks make it easier for operators and customers to reason about neoclouds as a coherent category and for new entrants to position themselves within it.

## What’s Happening?

The current momentum in the Neocloud Operators category can be viewed through three lenses: the macro trajectory of AI infrastructure spending and GPU pricing; the defining events that have crystallized the category; and the concentration and structure of capital flowing into neocloud assets and platforms.

### CAGR and TAM Dynamics

On the macro level, AI infrastructure is now recognized as one of the fastest‑growing segments of global IT spending, and neocloud operators are increasingly capturing a share of that growth. IDC’s Worldwide Quarterly Artificial Intelligence Infrastructure Tracker indicates that organizations increased spending on AI compute and storage hardware by 166 percent year‑over‑year in Q2 2025, with servers containing embedded accelerators accounting for 91.8 percent of server AI infrastructure spending. [^8f9tye] The same tracker forecasts that accelerated servers will grow at a five‑year CAGR of 42 percent and will represent over 95 percent of server AI infrastructure spending by 2029, underscoring the centrality of GPU‑rich architectures in future data centers. [^8f9tye] Complementing this, IDC’s broader AI infrastructure spending analysis estimates a five‑year CAGR of approximately 31 percent from 2025 to 2029, with total AI infrastructure spend surpassing $1 trillion in some scenarios and reaching at least $758 billion in its baseline forecast. [^wncds7] [^8f9tye] This discrepancy between $758 billion and “over $1 trillion” reflects differences in methodology and scope—for example, whether certain cloud services and software layers are included—but both figures affirm that AI infrastructure is on a trajectory to become a trillion‑dollar market in which neoclouds can carve out substantial niches. [^wncds7] [^8f9tye]

Within this macro landscape, neocloud GPU rental pricing has undergone a notable evolution. SemiAnalysis observes that while some commentators characterize H100 rental prices as “collapsing,” the firm interprets the trend as a logical decline in the cost of computing as H100 supply improves across both hyperscalers and neoclouds. [^30zj83] Their GPU Cloud Economics and AI Neocloud Anatomy reports, reiterated in the ClusterMAX analysis, detail how neocloud giants such as CoreWeave and Nebius have used early access to new GPU allocations—often in partnership with NVIDIA—to deploy clusters rapidly and lock in long‑term, low‑risk contracts with favorable customers, securing strong internal rates of return (IRR) on their GPU investments. [^30zj83] At the same time, more than 100 neoclouds and hyperscalers now provide widespread GPU rental availability, and many neoclouds bundle ancillary costs into all‑inclusive hourly rates that remain well below hyperscaler prices. [^q5yz1o] [^30zj83] Runpod’s pricing table illustrates this competitive landscape, with community cloud access to H100 PCIe GPUs at $1.99 per hour, H200 GPUs at $3.59 per hour, and A100 PCIe GPUs at $1.19 per hour, while secure cloud variants add only $0.10–$0.40 per hour for SOC 2 compliance and dedicated infrastructure. [^fru0mo] These figures highlight how neocloud operators can offer enterprise‑grade GPU access at a fraction of hyperscaler rates, while adjusting their pricing models—for example, via serverless “flex workers” and “active workers” tiers—to optimize utilization and margin. [^fru0mo]

### Category-Creation Events

Several events between 2022 and 2026 have crystallized the neocloud category, giving it distinct reference implementations and signaling legitimization by both operators and analysts. One foundational narrative is Crusoe’s origin story, which recounts how the company built “a new kind of cloud — not as a follower in a crowded market, but as a ‘Neocloud’ born from a unique moment in time, with a fundamentally different approach.” [^7jowi2] Crusoe Cloud began operations in mid‑2022 with a modest deployment of A100 servers in a containerized data center in Montana; by December 2023, it had expanded into new data centers in Virginia and Texas, increasing GPU capacity and network egress; and by April 2025, it launched its first large‑scale climate‑aligned compute facility in Iceland powered entirely by renewable energy. [^7jowi2] The company then introduced Crusoe Spark in June 2025 as a turnkey modular AI data‑center solution designed to rapidly deploy powerful, low‑latency compute to any location. [^7jowi2] This sequence of launches and expansions provides a concrete blueprint for how a vertically integrated neocloud can scale from a niche deployment to a global footprint while maintaining a climate‑aligned mission.

Voltage Park’s launch of its AI Factory in 2025 similarly marks a defining product event in the category. [^4umvjt] The AI Factory is described as a “fully integrated hardware and software platform” that enables enterprises to deploy and scale customized AI systems quickly, avoiding the learning curve, high costs, data‑privacy trade‑offs, and vendor lock‑in associated with current AI infrastructure platforms. [^4umvjt] Voltage Park positions the AI Factory as use‑case driven, with a minimal, modular, end‑to‑end stack that can scale seamlessly as needs evolve, offering full security and privacy without model or vendor lock‑in, and a model‑agnostic, compute‑agnostic design capable of running any open or closed model on any infrastructure. [^4umvjt] The next phase of the AI Factory will introduce a self‑service Drag‑and‑Drop interface for configuring AI systems using pre‑built “Blueprints,” further reducing friction between experimentation and production. [^4umvjt] In effect, the AI Factory turns Voltage Park from a pure GPU rental provider into an AI systems platform, pushing the neocloud category up the stack towards full AI “factories” rather than raw compute.

Together AI’s announcement that it is co‑building one of the world’s largest optimized GPU clusters, featuring 36,000 NVIDIA GB200 NVL72 GPUs in partnership with Hypertec Cloud starting in Q1 2025, is another major milestone. [^ynlu1a] The cluster combines Blackwell Tensor Core GPUs, Grace CPUs, NVLink, InfiniBand, and the Together Kernel Collection—an optimized software suite developed by Together’s co‑founder and FlashAttention creator Tri Dao—to deliver high‑performance infrastructure for training, fine‑tuning, and inference of large generative models. [^ynlu1a] Together AI frames this project as creating an “AI acceleration supercloud,” pulling together distributed cloud GPUs into a developer‑friendly platform that maximizes performance and scales to meet exponential demand. [^ynlu1a] The sheer scale of 36,000 GB200 GPUs and secured capacity for over 100,000 GPUs throughout 2025 demonstrates that neocloud operators can now build clusters rivaling or exceeding hyperscaler deployments, and in doing so, they anchor the category in visible, headline‑making infrastructure.

On the financial and analyst‐naming side, McKinsey’s 2025 article “The evolution of neoclouds and their next moves” explicitly labels and analyzes neoclouds as a distinct wave of GPU cloud providers, situating them in relation to hyperscalers, chipmakers, and AI startups. [^q5yz1o] The piece notes that neoclouds were originally stopgaps to address the GPU shortage, but have since evolved into more structured businesses with footprints across multiple regions and backing from venture capital, private equity, and sovereign wealth funds. [^q5yz1o] SemiAnalysis’s “AI Neocloud Anatomy and Playbook” and the ClusterMAX GPU Cloud Rating System further institutionalize the category by providing dedicated frameworks for evaluating neocloud economics, reliability, and security, as well as by discussing neocloud IRR, GPU rental pricing trends, and debates around demand and capacity. [^6gpbyh] [^30zj83] Together, these reports and narratives ensure that “neocloud” is not merely marketing jargon but an analyst‑recognized category with definitional and methodological content.

### Capital Concentration and Financing Structures

Capital in the neocloud category is increasingly concentrated in a combination of large debt facilities tied to contracted cash flows and equity investments in platform and infrastructure build‑outs. Lambda’s $1.008 billion investment‑grade term loan is a prime example of how capital providers now view GPU clusters as bankable assets. [^nson9i] The facility was oversubscribed, priced within the targeted range, and marked Lambda’s third new credit market opened in 18 months, following another billion‑dollar broadly syndicated loan in August 2026. [^nson9i] CEO Michel Combes emphasized that this capital “underwrites infrastructure over decades rather than quarters” and is secured by long‑term customer contracts, indicating a shift from venture‑driven short‑term capacity expansions to utility‑like infrastructure financing. [^nson9i]

Nebius’s $775 million senior secured debt facility, announced in July 2026, similarly converts existing operational infrastructure assets into growth capital. [^lc2qme] The transaction is backed by deployed GPU infrastructure and contracted cash flows with an investment‑grade customer, with the facility and underlying contract together covering more than 100 percent of the capital expenditure required to deploy the GPUs. [^lc2qme] Nebius notes that it already has more than $40 billion of additional contracted revenue from investment‑grade customers such as Microsoft and Meta, and expects to raise further capital on similar terms. [^lc2qme] The deal was significantly oversubscribed and led by MUFG as structuring agent, sole bookrunner, and underwriter, suggesting strong institutional appetite for AI‑infrastructure‑linked securities. [^lc2qme]

Crusoe’s $175 million credit facility from Victory Park Capital to expand its Iceland data‑center capacity points to a parallel pattern of capital formation focused on climate‑aligned AI infrastructure. [^mgvm0v] The expansion at atNorth’s ICE02 data center incorporates NVIDIA DGX GB200 NVL72 instances, Blackwell GPUs, and direct liquid‑to‑chip cooling, and is powered 100 percent by geothermal and hydro energy. [^mgvm0v] Victory Park Capital’s statement highlights Crusoe’s “unique positioning” to develop and operate AI‑optimized data centers that efficiently support large‑scale GPU clusters, framing the investment as exposure to both energy and AI infrastructure. [^mgvm0v] Crusoe’s collaboration with AMD to deploy Instinct MI355X GPUs within its global network of data centers, announced in June 2025, adds an equity‑like dimension, aligning the company with a second major chip ecosystem and expanding its addressable market beyond NVIDIA‑centric workloads. [^p7agdw]

Beyond these headline financings, SemiAnalysis notes that neocloud giants and emerging neoclouds often rely on spot pricing, pre‑uploading of large datasets, and no‑egress policies to improve their economics and attract customers. [^30zj83] Spot pricing allows neoclouds to sell idle compute capacity at discounted rates with clauses permitting reclamation if higher‑paying workloads arise, smoothing revenue and utilization. [^30zj83] Pre‑uploading datasets avoids wasting GPU time on transfers, improving effective revenue per GPU hour. [^30zj83] All‑inclusive pricing bundles CPU, networking, power, local storage, and orchestration into the GPU rental fee, simplifying billing and making margins more predictable. [^30zj83] These operational and pricing strategies are the microeconomic counterpart to the macro capital structures described above, and together they define the financial logic of neocloud operators.

## Market Incumbents

In the context of Neocloud Operators, **incumbents** are the large, well‑capitalized entities whose infrastructure, chips, or contracts already anchor significant portions of AI compute, and whose offerings or partnerships shape the economics and expectations of the category. These include neocloud giants that operate massive GPU clusters, chipmakers whose accelerators form the core of those clusters, and hyperscaler‑aligned firms whose demand underwrites asset‑level financing.
