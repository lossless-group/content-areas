# Datacenter Builders

The category of **Datacenter Builders** has rapidly become one of the critical bottlenecks and enablers of digital infrastructure, sitting at the intersection of construction, engineering, and cloud-scale computing. At its core, this market comprises the general contractors, EPC firms, specialist engineers and niche developers who design, build, commission, and increasingly help finance the physical facilities that host modern IT infrastructure, from hyperscale cloud campuses to edge and modular sites. [^3bccsm] [^dj6fr7] [^4ylq3b] As artificial intelligence, cloud services and colocation demand surge, data center construction has evolved from a conventional commercial building niche into a distinct global market with its own leading firms, specialized delivery models, and capital structures. [^op7p69] [^7acldk] [^g4wu45]

The global data center construction market is already vast: Grand View Research estimates it was valued at approximately USD 261.3 billion in 2025 and projects growth to USD 662.7 billion by 2033, implying a compound annual growth rate of 12.7% from 2026 to 2033. [^op7p69] Fortune Business Insights offers a similar but somewhat larger view, pegging the market at USD 275.16 billion in 2025 and forecasting USD 761.19 billion by 2034 at a CAGR of 11.97%. [^7acldk] Against this backdrop, North America alone commands nearly 38–42% of global construction spend depending on the source, and the United States accounts for roughly one-third of worldwide revenue. [^op7p69] [^7acldk] [^g4wu45] [^8cyyy1] This profile examines the Datacenter Builders category as it stands in the mid‑2020s, focusing on how the market is defined, why it has coalesced so sharply in the past few years, what its current momentum looks like in numbers and landmark projects, and which firms – incumbents, challengers, and innovators – are shaping its trajectory.

## Snapshot

_The Datacenter Builders category captures the ecosystem of contractors, engineering firms and specialist developers responsible for turning land, power and capital into operational data centers that can host cloud, AI and enterprise workloads. [^3bccsm] [^dj6fr7] [^4ylq3b] It is where construction-scale risk management meets mission‑critical uptime requirements, making it one of the most strategically important segments of the digital infrastructure stack._

> “The global data center construction market size was valued at USD 261.3 billion in 2025 and is projected to grow from USD 287.5 billion in 2026 to USD 662.7 billion by 2033, at a CAGR of 12.7% from 2026 to 2033.” [^op7p69]

In practical terms, Datacenter Builders are the companies that own or manage the full lifecycle of data center projects, from site feasibility and design through civil works, mechanical and electrical fit‑out, commissioning and sometimes ongoing operations support. [^c6i9bq] [^2uw1ai] [^38ye0u] [^dj6fr7] This profile covers the period from approximately 2024 through the early 2030s, aligning with the forecast horizons in major market reports and the current wave of hyperscale and AI‑factory developments documented in industry and financial coverage. [^op7p69] [^7acldk] [^t2v34n] [^qoqv47] It is worth a dedicated reference card now because the pace and scale of capital deployment into data center construction are unprecedented, the category’s boundaries are being actively contested, and a new generation of innovators and integrated builders is emerging alongside long‑standing engineering and construction giants. [^w580ae] [^du83y8] [^1l71th] [^yqswn1] [^4ylq3b]

## What is this Market Category?

The Datacenter Builders market category can be defined as the global set of firms that design, engineer, construct, and commission physical data center facilities, including the building shell, supporting infrastructure, and embedded services required to deliver an operational site capable of hosting IT equipment. [^3bccsm] [^g4wu45] [^gj26q3] Mordor Intelligence explicitly defines the data center construction market as the total value of greenfield builds combining civil works, electrical and mechanical fit‑outs, and embedded services needed to deliver an operational facility that can house IT infrastructure, which captures the core of this category. [^3bccsm] In practice, this includes general contractors and EPC firms such as AECOM, Turner Construction, DPR Construction, Holder Construction and Skanska; specialized mechanical and electrical builders like Mercury Engineering; and integrated design‑build entities such as Data Centre Builders in the UK, ITAC and ARCO National Construction in North America. [^t2v34n] [^g4wu45] [^38ye0u] [^w580ae] [^dj6fr7] [^4ylq3b] These firms solve complex problems for hyperscale cloud providers, colocation operators, telecom carriers, governments and large enterprises seeking secure, reliable and scalable digital infrastructure. [^2uw1ai] [^38ye0u] [^dj6fr7] [^vtoj1c]

The primary customers for Datacenter Builders are owners and operators of mission‑critical facilities, including hyperscale cloud platforms, multi‑tenant colocation providers, enterprise IT departments, and in some cases AI‑focused infrastructure developers. [^2uw1ai] [^38ye0u] [^dj6fr7] [^du83y8] [^qoqv47] For example, BuilderMuse notes that Holder Construction has become one of the most important general contractors in the data center market, building more hyperscale square footage than any other GC in North America and serving as general contractor for Microsoft, Google and Meta, while Mortenson and DPR Construction similarly serve major cloud and enterprise clients. [^du83y8] [^vtoj1c] ARCO National Construction’s portfolio includes projects for Digital Realty Trust, TierPoint, and a 200‑megawatt AI factory for Nebius, illustrating how builders serve both traditional colocation customers and newer AI‑focused developers. [^38ye0u] UK‑based Data Centre Builders positions itself as a specialist contractor that designs and builds hyperscale, multi‑tenant, edge and modular data centers for public‑ and private‑sector developers, co‑location providers, and enterprises seeking to grow their digital footprint. [^dj6fr7] Across these examples, the category’s central purpose is to translate demand for compute and storage into physical capacity with stringent uptime, energy and security requirements.

It is important to draw boundaries around what Datacenter Builders excludes. The category does not primarily encompass data center operators whose core business is leasing capacity, such as Equinix or Digital Realty, even though those operators often commission and oversee construction projects. [^38ye0u] [^4ylq3b] Nor does it include pure‑play IT equipment vendors, such as server manufacturers or software providers, whose products ultimately reside inside data centers but do not participate directly in the construction of facilities. [^op7p69] [^7acldk] Similarly, the market segment of power and cooling equipment manufacturers like Schneider Electric, Vertiv, ABB, Siemens and Eaton is adjacent but distinct; these firms supply critical components into projects led by Datacenter Builders but are typically classified under data center infrastructure or power and cooling rather than construction per se, even though some market reports list them as key players. [^7acldk] [^g4wu45] [^4ylq3b] Finally, traditional commercial builders who occasionally execute data center projects but lack dedicated mission‑critical practices may not be considered core members of this category, given the specialized expertise and repeat engagement that characterize true Datacenter Builders. [^w580ae] [^du83y8] [^1l71th]

The boundary is fuzzy and actively disputed in several directions. One contested edge concerns whether data center developers that design, build and operate facilities – such as Compass Datacenters, which BuilderMuse highlights as both a developer and builder challenging the traditional GC model – should be considered Datacenter Builders or part of the operator category. [^du83y8] Another involves whether vendors of [[content-areas/AI-Factories-Datacenters/Concepts/Integrated Electrical and Mechanical Systems]], including [[content-areas/AI-Factories-Datacenters/Organizations/Schneider Electric]] and [[Vertiv]], belong within Datacenter Builders because many market reports treat them as construction players, while practitioners often see them as infrastructure OEMs. [^7acldk] [^g4wu45] [^4ylq3b] There is also emerging debate about the inclusion of novel delivery models such as floating data centers being developed by startups like Mousterian, which combine civil, marine and data center engineering and blur the line between builder and operator. [^yqswn1] These disputes underscore that Datacenter Builders is not a static label but an evolving conceptual category reflecting changes in how digital infrastructure is financed, delivered and managed.

## Why Now?

The Datacenter Builders category has become coherent and expandable in the current period because several powerful forces have aligned across technology, demand, regulation and capital markets. A first enabling condition is the explosive growth of cloud computing, artificial intelligence and data‑intensive applications, which has driven unprecedented demand for new data center capacity worldwide. [^1l71th] [^vtoj1c] [^qoqv47] [^4ylq3b] Blackridge Research emphasizes that top data center construction companies such as AECOM, Holder Construction, Jacobs, Turner Construction, DPR Construction, and [[content-areas/AI-Factories-Datacenters/Organizations/Fluor]] are helping hyperscale operators design and build massive facilities capable of supporting AI and cloud services, including multi‑hundred‑megawatt campuses in Spain, Portugal and the United States. [^1l71th] TechSci Research similarly frames leading contractors like Turner, Holder, DPR and Mortenson as central to delivering hyperscale projects for [[organizations/Meta|Meta]], [[organizations/Google|Google]] and [[Tooling/Software Development/Cloud Infrastructure/Amazon Web Services|AWS]] under fast‑track timelines and complex mechanical, electrical and plumbing coordination, highlighting how demand from big tech has reshaped construction pipelines. [^vtoj1c] This sustained workload demand has transformed data center construction from an occasional specialized project type into a continuous global program of capacity expansion.

A second force is the maturation and specialization of construction delivery models and engineering practices tailored to mission‑critical facilities. Firms like [[ITAC]], [[ARCO National Construction]], [[Gray]] and Data Centre Builders have developed integrated design‑build approaches that combine engineering, self‑perform construction and advanced power systems expertise to deliver greenfield builds, brownfield expansions and retrofits with single‑source accountability. [^2uw1ai] [^38ye0u] [^dj6fr7] [^n158t0] ITAC describes its data center engineering and construction services as providing hyperscale, colocation and enterprise operators a single accountable partner from first line on a drawing to final commissioning, leveraging nearly four decades of mission‑critical project execution since 1988. [^2uw1ai] ARCO positions itself as a design‑build general contractor specializing in innovative, efficient and highly secure facilities and highlights a portfolio of LEED‑certified projects for Digital Realty Trust, TierPoint and Nebius. [^38ye0u] Data Centre Builders in London emphasizes its role as main contractor supervising every stage of the process, from early feasibility and design to construction, commissioning and handover, including modular construction and consolidation upgrades. [^c6i9bq] [^dj6fr7] The emergence of such dedicated mission‑critical practices has made it viable to treat Datacenter Builders as a distinct market category rather than an incidental subset of commercial construction.

A third enabling force is regulatory and societal pressure around energy efficiency, sustainability and resilience. Data centers are power‑intensive facilities, and there is growing scrutiny from governments and communities around their energy footprint, water use and carbon emissions. [^op7p69] [^7acldk] [^g4wu45] [^1l71th] Market reports emphasize that energy‑efficient design, innovative cooling infrastructure and sustainable construction practices are increasingly differentiating builders and influencing project awards. [^op7p69] [^7acldk] [^t2v34n] [^g4wu45] The mechanical infrastructure segment, which includes cooling systems and other energy‑related components, dominated the data center construction market with around 37% share in 2025 according to Global Market Insights, and is expected to grow at a 5.8% CAGR through 2035, indicating continued investment in advanced mechanical solutions. [^t2v34n] Innovators such as Mousterian explicitly emphasize designs that require far less energy and do not use municipal groundwater, leveraging floating or water‑adjacent facilities to mitigate environmental impact. [^yqswn1] These regulatory and societal forces push the category to adopt new technologies and construction methodologies, reinforcing its distinctiveness.

A fourth force underpinning the category’s coherence is capital formation and financial structuring tailored to large‑scale data center projects. CBRE’s “North America Data Center Trends H2 2025” report highlights that data center investment activity has been driven by sustained M&A, structured financing for AI‑factory mega‑campuses, and record issuance of single‑asset single‑borrower commercial mortgage‑backed securities (SASB CMBS) backed by data center deals. [^qoqv47] The report notes that annual SASB CMBS issuance reached USD 11.2 billion, with data center deals accounting for roughly 11% of total issuance, and that more than USD 8 billion of CoreWeave‑leased financing supported build‑to‑suit construction projects in H2 2025. [^qoqv47] Meta’s joint venture with Blue Owl Capital to finance its USD 27 billion Hyperion data center campus in Louisiana and the expansion of OpenAI, Oracle and SoftBank’s Stargate project to nearly 7 gigawatts of planned capacity and more than USD 400 billion in investment over three years epitomize how capital is being targeted at hyperscale developments. [^qoqv47] As capital allocators and lenders increasingly recognize data centers as an institutional asset class, builders who can deliver such mega‑projects reliably become central actors, solidifying the market category.

A fifth enabling condition is the emergence of specialized ecosystem recognition and benchmarking, which gives analytical shape to the category. Multiple industry analyses and rankings explicitly identify top data center construction companies and design‑build engineering firms, differentiating them from general construction players. [^w580ae] [^du83y8] [^1l71th] [^vtoj1c] [^4ylq3b] Data Centre Magazine’s “Top 10: Design, Build & Engineering Companies” and TechSci Research’s “Top 11 Data Center Construction Companies in the World” both profile firms based on their data center capabilities and revenue, treating mission‑critical construction as a distinct competency. [^w580ae] [^vtoj1c] BuilderMuse’s “Top 20 Data Center Construction Contractors in 2026” goes further by estimating annual data center revenues for GCs such as Holder, DPR, Mortenson and Turner, implicitly sizing a sub‑segment of construction activity. [^du83y8] Spherical Insights’ list of the top 25 companies in the global data center construction market likewise frames firms in relation to their role in hyperscale and mission‑critical projects. [^4ylq3b] This growing industry discourse and quantitative benchmarking both reflect and reinforce the conceptual coherence of Datacenter Builders as a standalone category.

## What's Happening?

### CAGR and TAM

The current momentum of the Datacenter Builders category is best understood through the divergent yet consistently bullish market size and growth estimates produced by several analyst firms. Grand View Research estimates that the global data center construction market was worth USD 261.3 billion in 2025 and projects growth to USD 662.7 billion by 2033, implying a CAGR of 12.7% from 2026 to 2033. [^op7p69] Fortune Business Insights offers a somewhat larger TAM, valuing the market at USD 275.16 billion in 2025 and forecasting USD 761.19 billion by 2034, with a slightly lower CAGR of 11.97% over the forecast period. [^7acldk] Precedence Research presents a slightly different baseline, estimating the market size at USD 258.56 billion in 2025 and projecting growth to around USD 524.37 billion by 2035 at a CAGR of 7.33% from 2026 to 2035. [^g4wu45] Global Market Insights, Mordor Intelligence and Market Research Future present more conservative growth trajectories, with Global Market Insights valuing the market at USD 227.6 billion in 2025 and forecasting USD 434.7 billion by 2035 at a 6.8% CAGR, Mordor projecting USD 431.39 billion by 2031 from a 2025 base of USD 281.34 billion at 7.51% CAGR, and Market Research Future estimating USD 557.5 billion by 2035 from USD 255.7 billion in 2025 at 7.82% CAGR. [^t2v34n] [^3bccsm] [^gj26q3] Taken together, these figures suggest a broad consensus that the market will roughly double or triple over the next decade, even though individual forecasts differ in baseline sizing and growth rates.

These differences partly reflect varying definitions and methodological choices. Mordor Intelligence explicitly defines the data center construction market as the total value of greenfield builds that combine civil works, electrical and mechanical fit‑outs, and embedded services needed to deliver an operational facility, thereby focusing on new builds rather than retrofits or expansions. [^3bccsm] Market Research Future similarly scopes its analysis to global construction of data center facilities, including electrical, mechanical, general construction and associated design/commissioning services, with market size checkpoints showing USD 255.7 billion in 2025, USD 276.4 billion in 2026 and USD 557.5 billion by 2035. [^gj26q3] In contrast, Grand View Research and Fortune Business Insights appear to adopt broader definitions that may encompass a wider range of project types, contributing to higher TAM estimates. [^op7p69] [^7acldk] Precedence Research’s mid‑range sizing and growth rates, along with its explicit breakdown of U.S. and North American shares, suggest a balanced methodology that integrates multiple segments. [^g4wu45] For an analyst or operator using this category profile, the disagreement among credible sources is itself information: it indicates that while the direction of growth is clear, the underlying construction value is sensitive to what is counted as “data center construction” and which geographies and project types are included.

The geographic distribution of TAM underscores the centrality of North America and, within it, the United States. Grand View Research reports that North America dominated the global market with a revenue share of 40.9% in 2025, while Fortune Business Insights cites a slightly lower share of 38% for North America in the same year. [^op7p69] [^7acldk] Precedence Research estimates that North America held the largest market share of 42% in 2025 and notes that the U.S. data center construction market was valued at USD 96.60 billion in 2025, projected to reach approximately USD 200.27 billion by 2035 at a 7.56% CAGR. [^g4wu45] Grand View’s U.S. outlook similarly notes that the U.S. accounted for 36% of the global market in 2025, with revenue of USD 94,141.4 million and a forecast to reach USD 211,657.8 million by 2033 at an 11% CAGR. [^8cyyy1] These figures reinforce that Datacenter Builders serving the U.S. and broader North American region are working in the world’s most important demand center, even as Europe and Asia–Pacific see significant growth and emerging markets such as Mexico are identified as the fastest growing regional markets in North America. [^t2v34n] [^g4wu45] [^8cyyy1] For category framing, this means that many of the most strategic projects, clients and capital flows described in subsequent sections are oriented around North American hyperscale and colocation activity.

### Category Creation Events

Several defining events and developments have crystallized the Datacenter Builders category in industry and financial discourse. One important set of category‑creation moments comes from the emergence of ranked lists and profiles of leading data center construction firms. Data Centre Magazine’s “Top 10: Design, Build & Engineering Companies” highlights Future‑Tech, Collen Construction, Mercury, HITT Contracting, DPR Construction, Mace, Turner Construction, Skanska, Jacobs Solutions and AECOM as key players enhancing the construction and optimization of modern data centers, with specific revenue figures and mission‑critical experience cited. [^w580ae] TechSci Research’s “Top 11 Data Center Construction Companies in the World” similarly profiles Turner Construction, Holder Construction, DPR Construction, Mortenson and others as trusted names in large‑scale infrastructure and mission‑critical construction, emphasizing their work with tech giants like Meta, Google, AWS and Microsoft. [^vtoj1c] Blackridge Research’s “Top 10 Data Center Construction Companies 2026” and Spherical Insights’ “Top 25 Companies in Global Data Center Construction Market” further codify a leading cohort of builders such as AECOM, Holder, Jacobs, Turner, DPR, Fluor, Mortenson, EQUANS, Collen and Mercury, explicitly linking them to hyperscale projects and AI‑ready facilities. [^1l71th] [^4ylq3b] These analyses collectively mark the recognition of Datacenter Builders as a discrete strategic group within global construction.

The second class of category‑defining events centers on specific hyperscale and AI‑factory projects that showcase the scale and complexity of modern data center construction. Blackridge Research notes that AECOM was selected by Nostrum Data Centers to lead design and construction management for the EUR 1.9 billion Nostrum Evergreen data center project in Badajoz, Spain, expected to reach 500 megawatts of capacity. [^1l71th] The same source reports AECOM serving as general contractor for DataBank’s IAD5 campus in Culpeper, Virginia, a 192‑megawatt, 1.4 million square foot hyperscale facility, and supporting Start Campus in the development of the SINES DC campus in Portugal, a USD 9.4 billion hyperscale project expected to deliver 1.2 gigawatts of IT capacity powered entirely by renewable energy. [^1l71th] ARCO National Construction’s portfolio includes the Nebius AI factory in Independence, Missouri, a 488,000 square foot, 200‑megawatt development under construction, which demonstrates the emergence of AI‑focused “factories” as a distinct data center type. [^38ye0u] These mega‑projects are not simply large buildings; they are complex, multi‑year efforts that push the limits of power availability, cooling, sustainability and construction logistics, requiring highly specialized builders and reinforcing the category’s strategic visibility.

A third cluster of category‑creation events involves novel delivery models and infrastructural concepts that expand what counts as a data center and who builds it. The Dallas News article on Mousterian describes a Dallas startup developing data centers that float on water, with a self‑funded, 10‑person team handling design, engineering and development for floating, land‑based and water‑adjacent facilities. [^yqswn1] Mousterian’s founder characterizes the company as a high‑performance data center developer with unique facility offerings that require far less energy and do not use municipal groundwater, underscoring a contrarian thesis about sustainable data center siting and resource use. [^yqswn1] This kind of innovation challenges traditional construction paradigms and invites rethinking of what “building a data center” entails. Similarly, Compass Datacenters, highlighted by BuilderMuse as both a developer and builder that designs, builds and operates standardized, repeatable data centers, challenges the conventional division between owners and general contractors by internalizing construction capabilities. [^du83y8] These examples illustrate how the category is being stretched by new entrants and models, even as incumbent GCs remain central.

Finally, the financial and capital‑markets events documented by CBRE contribute to category creation by recognizing data centers as a distinct asset class. CBRE’s H2 2025 North America report notes that an uptick in year‑end transaction activity and large‑scale development completions have driven demand for construction financing, joint‑venture equity and forward commitments for new data centers. [^qoqv47] It highlights that annual investment volume for operational data centers fell by nearly 50% year‑over‑year to approximately USD 3 billion, even as debt, joint ventures and equity investment remained active and SASB CMBS issuance for data center deals hit all‑time highs. [^qoqv47] Major ventures such as Meta’s joint venture with Blue Owl Capital to finance its USD 27 billion Hyperion campus and the expansion of OpenAI, Oracle and SoftBank’s Stargate project to nearly 7 gigawatts of planned capacity and more than USD 400 billion in investment over three years underscore that building data centers at this scale is inseparable from sophisticated financial engineering. [^qoqv47] By tying data center construction directly to capital markets and institutional investment narratives, such events help define Datacenter Builders as not merely “contractors” but key actors in a broader financialized infrastructure ecosystem.

### Capital Concentration

Capital is increasingly concentrating around Datacenter Builders in several ways: through the revenues of leading contractors, the financing structures backing hyperscale projects, and the geographic focus of investment. BuilderMuse estimates that Holder Construction has USD 3–4 billion in annual data center revenue, making it arguably the single most important general contractor in the North American data center market. [^du83y8] The same source notes that Mortenson has built a massive data center practice with estimated annual data center revenue of USD 2–3 billion, and that DPR Construction, with USD 8 billion‑plus overall revenue, has become a dominant force in data center construction. [^du83y8] Data Centre Magazine provides revenue figures for other major players: Mercury recorded EUR 1.6 billion in 2023 turnover (approximately USD 1.77 billion) and positions itself as Europe’s most experienced enterprise data center service provider with more than 20 years of experience; HITT Contracting reported USD 5.4 billion in revenue in 2022; DPR Construction reported USD 9.6 billion in revenue in 2023; and Turner Construction, the largest domestic contractor in the United States, reported revenue of USD 14.41 billion in 2020. [^w580ae] These numbers indicate that data center work now contributes materially to the top line of major construction companies, with some building dedicated mission‑critical divisions.

From an industry concentration standpoint, Global Market Insights reports that AECOM led the data center construction market with over 3.5% share in 2025, and that the top five players – AECOM, Skanska, Jacobs Engineering, Obayashi and Kajima – collectively held around 12% market share. [^t2v34n] Extending the lens to the top seven companies, including NTT Facilities and DPR Construction alongside the core five, yields roughly 14% share. [^t2v34n] Precedence Research similarly notes that the market is dominated by top general contractors and technical builders such as Turner Construction Company, HITT Contracting, DPR Construction, Holder Construction, Skanska and Mercury Engineering, and lists major players including AECOM, Gensler, Arup Group and others. [^g4wu45] Market Research Future’s analysis identifies Turner Construction, DPR Construction, Holder Construction, AECOM, Exyte, Skanska, Mercury Engineering and JE Dunn Construction as key players in a market whose global size was USD 255.7 billion in 2025 and is projected to reach USD 557.5 billion by 2035. [^gj26q3] Spherical Insights’ top 25 list reinforces this concentration, naming AECOM, DPR, Turner, Holder, Jacobs, Fluor, Skanska, Structure Tone, Fortis, Gilbane, Suffolk, Mortenson, Mercury, Schneider Electric, Vertiv, Eaton, ABB, Siemens, Johnson Controls, Black & Veatch, Arup, HDR, Winthrop, Exyte and Larsen & Toubro as leading global companies. [^4ylq3b] The repeated presence of a core set of firms across these analyses underscores that capital, contracts and market power are heavily concentrated in a relatively small group of large builders.

On the financing side, CBRE’s H2 2025 report shows capital clustering around specific project types and sponsors. It notes that data center deals accounted for approximately 11% of total SASB CMBS issuance, a record share, and that annual issuance of SASB CMBS reached USD 11.2 billion. [^qoqv47] The report states that the financing market saw more than USD 8 billion of CoreWeave‑leased financing for build‑to‑suit construction projects in H2 2025, illustrating how long‑term compute demand from AI‑oriented tenants is being leveraged to secure project financing. [^qoqv47] It also points out that powered shell facilities – structures with power and basic infrastructure ready for fit‑out – continued to appeal to a wide range of investors, with numerous transactions concentrated in Northern Virginia. [^qoqv47] Furthermore, Meta’s joint venture with Blue Owl Capital for its USD 27 billion Hyperion campus and the expanded Stargate project backed by OpenAI, Oracle and SoftBank with over USD 400 billion of planned investment show that capital is not only concentrated in existing assets but also heavily committed to future construction pipelines. [^qoqv47] These financing patterns indicate that Datacenter Builders operating at the hyperscale end of the market are increasingly embedded in complex capital stacks, working alongside private equity, sovereign wealth funds and public‑market investors.

Geographically, capital concentration is most apparent in U.S. and North American markets, but Europe and selected emerging regions are quickly gaining prominence. Grand View’s U.S. outlook notes that the U.S. market generated USD 94,141.4 million in revenue in 2025 and is expected to reach USD 211,657.8 million by 2033, with the U.S. accounting for 36% of global data center construction revenue in 2025. [^8cyyy1] Precedence Research highlights that the U.S. market is predicted to grow from USD 96.60 billion in 2025 to around USD 200.27 billion by 2035, with Mexico identified as the fastest‑growing regional market in North America and projected to reach USD 3,560 million by 2033. [^g4wu45] [^8cyyy1] In Europe, mega‑projects such as Nostrum Evergreen in Spain and SINES DC Campus in Portugal, with capacities of 500 megawatts and 1.2 gigawatts respectively, represent substantial capital commitments to new hubs. [^1l71th] ARCO’s AI factory for Nebius in Missouri and its projects for TierPoint and Digital Crossroad show similar regional concentration within the United States. [^38ye0u] For Datacenter Builders, this distribution means that while North America remains the primary market, strategic opportunities are emerging in selected European corridors and high‑growth Latin American markets, requiring international capabilities and local partnerships.

## Market Incumbents

The incumbent tier of Datacenter Builders consists of large public companies, global construction and engineering giants, and mature private firms with extensive mission‑critical footprints and long operating histories. These are the organizations that enterprise and hyperscale buyers are most likely to already have contracts with in other domains, from infrastructure and industrial projects to commercial buildings, and that can credibly deliver multi‑hundred‑megawatt campuses across multiple geographies. [^t2v34n] [^g4wu45] [^w580ae] [^du83y8] [^1l71th] [^4ylq3b] They typically manage complex portfolios that include data centers alongside other sectors, but have developed specialized practices for mission‑critical facilities.

Among the most prominent incumbents is [AECOM](https://www.aecom.com), a global infrastructure consulting and construction company that provides design, engineering, project management and construction services for hyperscale, enterprise and mission‑critical data centers worldwide. [^t2v34n] [^1l71th] [^4ylq3b] Global Market Insights identifies AECOM as the market leader in data center construction with more than 3.5% share in 2025, and notes that it is part of the top five players collectively holding 12% of market share.
